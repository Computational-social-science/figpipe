## WHO'S ON FIRST IN THE CELLULAR RESPONSE TO DNA DAMAGE?

## Susan D. Cline and Philip C.Hanawalt

Cellular DNA-repair pathways involve proteins that have roles in other DNA-metabolic processes, as well as those that are dedicated to damage removal. Several proteins, which have diverse functions and are not known to have roles in DNA repair, also associate with damaged DNA. These newly discovered interactions could either facilitate or hinder the recognition of DNA damage, and so they could have important effects on DNA repair and genetic integrity. The outcome for the cell, and ultimately for the organism, might depend on which proteins arrive first at sites of DNA damage.

The maintenance ofDNA integrity is vital for the viability of cells and the health of organisms.The genome is under constant attack from endogenous metabolic byproducts and environmental factors that can alter its chemical structure and corrupt its encoded message.The oxidation and alkylation of bases, formation of bulky chemical adducts and cross-linking of adjacent nucleotides occur thousands of times per day in the DNA of human cells. Such forms of damage must be removed to ensure complete and accurate DNA replication and transcription.

Various proteins have evolved to recognize DNA modifications and either repair them by direct reversal of the chemical alteration or target them for removal by the EXCISION REPAIR pathways (FIG. 1).However, proteins that are involved in other cellular processes can also bind altered DNA structures. These interactions might alter the course of DNA repair in three ways: by interfering with the damage removal by repair enzymes; by aiding damage recognition if the bound protein functions as an 'antenna' to attract repair enzymes to sites of damage; or by directing the damage to an alternative processing pathway (FIGS 2-5).

In this review,we first consider the dedicated DNArepair proteins that recognize DNA damage.We then discuss some classes of proteins with numerous roles that include DNA repair. Finally,we consider other proteins that interact with damaged DNA, and that might compete with, enhance or direct repair. The ultimate outcome for the cell depends on 'who's on first' in the damage response.

## DNA-repair proteins that recognize damage

In a cell, reactive chemical species that are produced during respiration,metabolism and biosynthesis bombard the genetic material and create a host of endogenous DNA lesions through the oxidation and alkylation of bases.The spontaneous deamination of cytosine and hydrolytic release of purines from the sugar-phosphate backbone also occur at significant rates under normal physiological conditions 1 .Many other forms of DNA damage - including DNA strand breaks and bulky chemical adducts - arise on environmental or clinical exposure to carcinogenic chemicals, radiation and chemotherapeutic drugs.

Many cellular pathways have evolved to maintain genomic integrity. These involve direct chemical reversal, joining of broken DNA strands and excision of damaged DNA bases. Because of space constraints, this review focuses mainly on the repair of damage by the excision pathways, and the effects that other cellular proteins have on the repair of damage that is targeted for removal by these mechanisms.However, a similar discussion could be applied to the recognition and repair of DNA strand breaks by single-strand-break repair mechanisms 2 , homologous recombination and non-homologous end-joining 3 .

These pathways all involve recognition of DNA damage (TABLE 1), removal of a single-stranded section of DNA that contains the damage, repair replication across the gap and restoration of DNA continuity by ligation of the repaired strand.After recognition, the steps in these repair mechanisms produce new structural DNA intermediates,which also represent forms of damage that could be bound by cellular proteins and must be processed by the next enzyme activity in the pathway (FIG. 1).

The only substantial difference between the three excision repair pathways is that MMR uses exonucleases to excise a long, single-stranded region containing the damage, starting from one incision in the damaged strand.By contrast,BER and NER use endonucleases to incise the strand very near to the damage for the excision of the damaged fragment.Although damage is recognized and repaired by one excision mechanism, these pathways can collaborate for the removal of some DNA alterations.

Base excision repair.Relatively minor modifications to the bases of DNA are removed by either short- or long-patch BER (for a review, see REF. 4). In BER, a DNA GLYCOSYLASEwhich has a high affinity for certain forms of base damage - binds selectively to an altered nucleotide and cleaves the N-glycosylic bond between the base and the sugar 4,5 (TABLE 1). The resultant abasic deoxyribose (dR) sugar (apyrimidinic or apurinic (AP) site) is processed further by AP LYASE, AP ENDONUCLEASE and DNA POLYMERASE activities to generate intermediates that contain a singlebase gap, a single-strand nick, or a single- or multiplenucleotide overhang (which is known as a flap) at a nick 4 (FIG. 1).

A tight coordination of BER enzymes is necessary to prevent the disruption of repair by the binding of other cellular factors to these DNA structures.Although no human syndromes have been attributed to inherited BER dysfunction, single-nucleotide polymorphisms in MYH- which encodes a glycosylase that recognizes A opposite 8-oxoguanine (8-oxoG) - have been linked to an increased susceptibility to colorectal cancer 6,7 . This lack of disease phenotype could indicate that there is an essential role for the BER pathway in dealing with DNA damage that occurs during development.

Nucleotide excision repair.The NER pathway is responsible for removing various bulky DNA lesions, such as benzo(a)pyrene diol epoxide (BPDE) adducts, which arise from environmental exposure to benzo(a)pyrene, and dimerized pyrimidine bases,which are induced by ultraviolet radiation (UV) from sunlight (for a review, see REF. 8).An acute sensitivity to UV light is a hallmark phenotype of two hereditary syndromes, XERODERMA PIGMENTOSUM (XP) and COCKAYNE'S SYNDROME,which result from defects in the early steps of NER. The nomenclature for human NER genes and proteins reflects the complementation groups for these diseases.

Bacteria recognize and excise bulky DNA modifications using the UvrABC complex. Eukaryotes, however, have evolved NER mechanisms comprising numerous protein complexes with conserved functions. In human cells, the XPC-HR23B complex (which is homologous to the yeast Rad4-Rad23 complex) in conjunction with XPA, the single-stranded-DNA-binding factor REPLICATION PROTEIN A (RPA) and the basal transcription factor complex TFIIH (which contains the helicases XPB and XPD) is responsible for the recognition and verification of DNA lesions and for recruiting other NER factors (see REF. 9 and references therein).Another factor,XPE, also assists in the identification of cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) 10 (TABLE 1).

The NER that occurs in silent regions of the genome and in the non-transcribed strands of active genes is known as global genomic repair. DNA damage in the transcribed strand, which blocks RNA polymerase (RNAP) elongation, is targeted for removal by a second pathway,which is known as TRANSCRIPTION-COUPLED REPAIR (TCR; for reviews, see REFS 11,12). Although there are fewer intermediates in NER than in BER (FIG.1), NER processes a structurally diverse repertoire of DNA modifications, and so it might have to compete with other proteins that interact with structural anomalies in DNA.

## Direct reversal of damage

Alkylation damage and UV-induced pyrimidine dimers can be repaired through direct chemical reversal by specialized enzymes (TABLE 1).However,whereas the mechanism for the direct repair of alkylation damage is conserved from bacteria to humans, humans lack the proteins that catalyse photoreversal.

In humans, three proteins have been identified that reverse alkylation damage. The O 6 -alkylguanine alkyltransferase catalyses the efficient transfer of alkyl substituents - which range in size from methyl to benzyl groups - from the O 6 of guanine to its own active-site cysteine residue 19 . The ABH2 and ABH3 proteins are dioxygenases, homologous to E. coli AlkB, which catalyse the oxidation and release of the methyl group from 1-methyladenine (1-meA) and 3-methylcytosine (3-meC) 20,21 . Some forms of alkylation damage can be repaired by the NER mechanisms in bacteria and eukaryotes,and the NER ofO 4 -methylthymine (O 4 -meT) is inhibited in mammalian cells by the binding of methyltransferases, which catalyse only a very slow methyl transfer from thymine 22 . Perhaps the slow repair by methyltransferase is sufficient for dealing with O 4 -meT, and methyltransferase-mediated inhibition of NER concentrates the efforts of the NER pathway on the removal of more abundant and deleterious forms of damage that are produced under alkylating conditions 23-25 .

In yeast, plants, and some bacteria, UV-induced CPDs (and in some cases 6-4 photoproducts; 6-4PPs) are also resolved directly by DNA photolyases through photoreactivation (for a review, see REF. 26). Photolyases bind tightly to CPDs and, on excitation by 340-400nm light, catalyse the cleavage of the cyclobutane linkage between the adjacent pyrimidines and restore the monomeric bases without cutting the phosphodiester backbone of DNA.Although this is obviously beneficial in sunlight, some photolyases can also assist the NER of CPDs in the absence of photoreactivating wavelengths 27,28 . Under photoreactivating conditions, both bacterial and yeast photolyase (Phr1) preferentially reverse CPDs in the non-transcribed strand of active genes 29,30 . Phr1 clears CPDs from the non-transcribed strand at a rate that is similar to that for the TCR of CPDs in the transcribed strand 31 . As Phr1 is upregulated in yeast by several DNAdamaging agents 32 , and can enhance the NER of CPDs when it is unable to resolve cyclobutane linkages directly, this enzyme functions as a model for proteins that interact with sites of DNA damage and have the potential to facilitate DNA-damage recognition by repair pathways (FIG. 3).

## Repair proteins with dual roles

Some of the proteins that are instrumental in the recognition and repair of DNA damage were identified initially by their other cellular functions. For example, RNAPs I and II and their associated basal transcription factor, TFIIH, are required for gene expression 33,34 , whereas RPA facilitates DNA replication by its association with the DNA polymerase-α PRIMASE 35 .The proteins that are involved in one pathway of DNA repair might also assist in other pathways of damage removal. In addition to the example of Phr1, MMR proteins are implicated in the TCR of UV damage 36-38 and bind to several chemically altered DNA bases.

The functional duality of these proteins indicates that other enzymes and enzyme complexes that have been well characterized biochemically might have extra roles in repair.Below we outline their cellular duties, then consider other proteins that interact with sites of DNA damage (TABLE 2) and their potential roles in repair (FIG. 2).

RNA polymerases. RNAPs function as recognition factors for the TCR of several forms of DNA damage 39 (for a review, see REF. 11).While translocating along DNA during transcriptional elongation, they might be arrested by the presence of damage in the template strand. Several bulky lesions,CPDs, BPDE adducts and CISPLATIN and psoralen cross-links arrest translocation of the rat liver RNAP II. So far, however, only CPDs and psoralen cross-links have been shown to arrest E. coli RNAP (for a review, see REF. 11).Although the arrest of any single RNAP by a particular form of damage depends on the stereochemistry and DNA-sequence context of the lesion 40,41 , the observation of RNAP arrest at a site-specific CPD lesion in vitro correlates with the TCR of this form of damage in both bacteria and human cells (see REF. 11 and references therein).

The mammalian RNAPs I and III do not elicit TCR 42,43 , but the RNAPI of Saccharomyces cerevisiae mediates the TCR of UV-induced DNA damage in ribosomal genes 39 . The requirement for the RNAP function as a damage-recognition factor is associated only with transcriptional arrest. Arrested RNAP complexes at unrepaired lesions evoke a strong signal for apoptosis in human cells 44 . Therefore, TCR initiation by RNAPII molecules not only signals for the repair of DNA lesions, but also prevents cell death by liberating arrested polymerases from sites of damage. So,mammalian RNAPs I and III either do not arrest at DNA damage, have an alternative mechanism for their release from and the repair of arresting lesions, or are not a signal for apoptosis when they are arrested during transcription. Further work is needed to determine the molecular criteria for TCR signalling by RNA polymerases.

RPA. RPA - the most abundant ssDNA-binding factor in human cells - is required for the assembly of the DNA polymerase-α/primase complex at the replication origin and the initiation of replication, as well as for the synthesis of nascent DNA 45 .Although its greatest affinity is for ssDNA 46 , RPA also binds readily to some types of DNA damage, including cisplatin 47-49 and psoralen cross-links 50 , and UV-induced lesions 51-53 (TABLE 2).RPA participates in NER through interactions with XPA, XPG and the XPF-ERCC1 complex. In association with XPA, RPA is necessary for the full opening of the DNA helix around sites of damage by TFIIH 54,55 . The RPA complex also seems to position the repair endonucleases for the incision of the damaged strand through interactions with XPG on its 3′-orientated side and XPF-ERCC1 on its 5′-facing end 56 .

If RPA binds first to the sites of damage in the cell, does this interfere with subsequent steps in the pathway? Or, as RPA is needed in these later steps, does its 'prebinding' at a lesion make the repair process more efficient? RPA has numerous protein associations and is a key component in many DNA-metabolic pathways, so how does it coordinate its involvement in so many different but simultaneous events? The phosphorylation of RPA subunits in response to DNA damage probably governs the transitions from cellular growth to DNA-repair functions.However, RPA associates with several repair factors in different repair pathways through its 32-kDa protein subunit 57 .Are there damage-specific phosphorylation sites on this subunit that control interactions with particular repair proteins? If RPA is first to bind to a site of DNA damage, can it interact specifically with repair factors that are appropriate for the repair of that lesion? These are questions that have yet to be addressed.

The physiological importance of the interactions of MMR proteins with NER proteins and chemical alterations in DNA was first suggested when both MutS and MutL were shown to be required for the TCR of CPDs in the Lac operon of E. coli 37 . Since then, roles in TCR for the eukaryotic homologues ofMutS and MutL have been implicated by the finding that human cells lacking functional MSH2 or MLH1 are deficient in the TCR of UV-damaged DNA 36,38 . ## Proteins with undefined roles in repair

The first step for all DNA-repair processes is lesion recognition (FIG. 2).Therefore, any protein that is associated with DNA damage in cells could affect the efficiency of damage removal. Several cellular factors that were not characterized for their role in repair have been shown to interact with various forms of DNA damage in vitroand/or in cells (TABLE 2).As many of these proteins have roles in other essential DNA-processing pathwayssuch as transcriptional regulation or topological maintenance of DNA - their binding to DNA lesions could indicate undiscovered functions for these factors in facilitating (FIG. 3), inhibiting (FIG. 4) or directing (FIG. 5) DNA-damage recognition.

It is unclear why and how the binding of these proteins - some of which share similar DNA-binding motifs - influence repair in different ways. HMGcontaining transcription factors might operate at similar regulatory sequences. If a site of damage occurs in the vicinity of these regulatory sequences,what determines which of these proteins will bind first, considering that the cellular consequences of each interaction could be different? Further complexity is added if the affinity of these transcription factors for DNA damage affects their 'normal' functions in gene expression.

The binding of transcription factors to cisplatininduced DNA adducts might affect their repair, but it might also sequester these proteins from their normal functions. For example, the human ribosomal RNA transcription factor (UBF) contains numerous HMG domains and shows the highest affinity of any known protein for 1,2-intrastand d(GpG) cisplatin adducts 88 . The treatment of cells with cisplatin 89 , or the presence of cisplatin-modified DNA in a reconstituted system 90 , seems to inhibit the activation of RNAPI transcription by UBF.DNA containing 1,2-d(GpG) cross-links can also lure TBP away from binding to the TATA box of the adenoviral promoter sequence 91 . This sequestering of transcription factors away from their roles in activation and initiation has been named 'transcription factor hijacking'by Essigman and co-workers 88 .

The hijacking of proteins by DNA damage is not limited to cisplatin adducts, because UV photoproducts also attract TBP and the TBP-free TAF(II) complex, TFTC 92,93 . In yeast, the loss of Nhp6A and Nhp6B confers a slight resistance to hydrogen peroxide and to UV irradiation,which indicates that HMG proteins might also interact with oxidative damage and UV photoproducts 87 . Likewise, hijacking might not only apply to transcription factors. HISTONE H1 binds more strongly than HMG1 to DNA containing intrastrand cisplatin adducts 94 . This is only one example of several proteins with other cellular roles - such as cytoskeletal formation, cell-cycle-checkpoint signalling and the maintenance of DNA topology - that also interact with sites of DNA damage (see below).

Fanconi anaemia proteins and spectrins. Cells from patients with FANCONI ANAEMIA (FA) are hypersensitive to DNA-cross-linking agents, ionizing radiation and oxygen (see REF. 95 and references therein). Through cellfusion experiments, eight complementation groups for FA have been identified - A, B, C,D1,D2, E, F and G - for which seven genes have been cloned: FANCA, BRCA2 (which has been identified for complementation groups B and D1), FANCC, FANCD2, FANCE, FANCF and FANCG 95 ). All of the FA proteins have homologues in mice, but only FANCD2 has known homologues in lower eukaryotes or bacteria.Although the FA proteins do not seem to be evolutionarily conserved and bear no sequence similarity to known DNArepair proteins, there is evidence that they participate in the repair of chemical interstrand DNA cross-links (ICLs) (for reviews, see REFS 95,96).

The mechanism for repair of ICLs in bacteria has been elucidated 97 , but the methods for repair of these lesions in eukaryotic cells remain unclear. ICLs are induced by chemotherapeutic agents (such as cisplatin, mitomycin C, nitrogen mustards and nitrosoureas), by malondialdehyde and by the many FUROCOUMARINS, such as psoralen,which occur naturally in certain plants and are also used medicinally (for a review, see REF. 96).Cell lines generated from Fanca, Fancc and Fancg null mice are hypersensitive to ICL-inducing agents and show chromosomal instability after exposure to these chemicals 98-100 . Recent data indicate that the FANCE protein interacts with both FANCC and FANCD2 and might transduce the signal from DNA-damage recognition by the FA nuclear complex to a downstream excision repair pathway that is mediated by the monoubiquitylation of FANCD2 (REFS 108,109).

ATR. The ATAXIA-TELANGIECTASIAmutated (ATM)-related protein,ATR, is a member of the phosphoinositide 3kinase-related kinase (PIKK) family, and is required for the mammalian checkpoint response to several forms of DNA damage (for a review, see REF. 113).ATR phosphorylates p53 and the CHK1 AND CHK2 kinases to initiate checkpoint signalling. The loss of ATR kinase activity renders cells sensitive to ionizing radiation, methyl methanesulphonate, hydroxyurea and UV 114 .The phosphorylation of p53 by ATR is stimulated by DNA, but it is stimulated to a greater extent by UV-irradiated DNA 115 . In fact, the presence of a single 6-4PP in DNA enhances binding by ATR, indicating that ATR might operate as a sensitive UV-damage sensor for checkpoint activation 115 .

The studies discussed above provide evidence that ATR might function not only as a kinase in the signalling cascade for the DNA-damage-checkpoint response, but also as a recognition factor for DNA damage that impedes DNA polymerase translocation. At present, there is no evidence that ATR interacts with excision repair proteins, and it is unlikely that repairrecognition enzymes signal for the DNA-damagecheckpoint response. So how is the checkpoint response initiated if repair-recognition enzymes or other proteins arrive at sites of damage before ATR? Does this indicate that there is a competition between DNA-repair pathways and the DNA-damage checkpoint for interaction with DNA damage?

The level of DNA damage that is sustained by the cell might be important. At low levels of damage, DNA-repair factors, which are highly specific for damage, could recognize and repair damage before its detection by the checkpoint proteins. However, if damage reaches a higher threshold, checkpoint proteins such as ATR could also find DNA lesions. The repair system might have been saturated and either needs time to work or cannot restore genomic integrity. If ATR signals for cell-cycle arrest, ATR might either dissociate from DNA to allow repair enzymes access to the lesion or participate in as-yetundiscovered interactions with excision repair factors to target the damage for repair.

Topoisomerases. The sensitivity of proliferating cells to the loss of DNA integrity is the basis for many therapeutic treatments for cancer. The local administration of ionizing radiation or infusion of drugs that produce extensive DNA damage can overwhelm the repair capacity of cancer cells, and eventually eliminate a tumour.Whereas radiation and cross-linking agents damage DNA directly, antineoplastic agents that are targeted to DNA TOPOISOMERASES exploit the normal mechanism of these enzymes to induce DNA strand breaks 119,120 . By enhancing the cleavage of the phosphodiester backbone or inhibiting re-ligation of the incised DNA, topoisomerase-targeted anticancer drugs increase levels of the (otherwise transient) covalent enzyme-DNA complexes that contain a protein-tethered DNA break. These tethers might be ruptured - converting the complexes to DNA strand breaks - when encountered by translocating replication 121-123 or transcriptional machinery 124,125 .

Studies with eukaryotic topoisomerases I and II have shown that several forms of DNA damage affect topoisomerase activity in a manner that is similar to that of enzyme-targeted drugs (TABLE 2).This list includes damage that is repaired by all of the excision repair pathways, as well as BER intermediates and deoxyribose sugar modifications. Topoisomerases do not seem to 'seek out' lesions in DNA, as topoisomerase-DNA cleavage sites do not always coincide with the location of DNA damage on oligonucleotide substrates in vitro. However, the presence of a single site of DNA damage near the position of enzyme-mediated DNA cleavage can increase the formation of topoisomerase-DNA complexes more than 20-fold, depending on the lesion (for a review, see REF. 126). In agreement with in vitro studies, the UV-irradiation of human fibroblasts 127 , the BPDE exposure of Chinese hamster V79 cells 128 , and the cytosine arabinoside 129 or gemcitabine 130 treatment of human leukaemia cells induces elevated levels of topoisomerase I-DNA complexes.

Owing to the fact that topoisomerases bound to DNA protect as many as 22 nucleotides, a site of DNA damage within a topoisomerase-DNA complex would probably be obscured from recognition by repair enzymes 131,132 . For the lesion to be repaired, the topoisomerase would have to facilitate lesion recognition and then be removed for subsequent damage excision. In yeast, overexpression of topoisomerase I (Top1) 133 or a loss of topoisomerase III (Top3) 134 confers hypersensitivity to methyl methanesulphonate,UV and ionizing radiation.Top3 enzymes have not been studied for their interaction with DNA damage.However, Top3 forms a complex with Sgs1, the yeast homologue of the E. coli RecQ helicase, which is needed for the intra-S-phase damage-checkpoint response and might resolve abnormal DNA structures arising in S phase due to damage 134 . An increase in Top1, on the other hand,might sensitize yeast to damaging agents by obscuring DNA lesions from repair factors.

As most topoisomerases in human cells are essential, the diversion of these enzymes from their primary duties in the maintenance of DNA topology might also be detrimental.With their DNA-nicking activity, topoisomerases might initiate cell-death programmes when the cell has experienced a threshold level of DNA damage, past which the genome is irreparable. In fact, in a myeloid leukaemia cell line, topoisomerase II (Top2) can mediate the excision of chromosomal loops after exposure to hydrogen peroxide 135 .These loops are similar in size to high-molecular-mass DNA fragments that are produced during apoptosis 136 , indicating that Top2 located at nuclear-matrix attachment sites might cleave DNA during cell death after an oxidative insult. Such a 'suicide'mechanism involving topoisomerases could be exploited by DNA-damaging and topoisomerasetargeted anticancer agents in eliminating malignant cells (an idea that was first proposed in REF. 126).At present, studies are under way in our laboratory to investigate the interaction of topoisomerases with DNA-damagerecognition proteins and the relationship between topoisomerase activity and the NER of UV-induced DNA damage in yeast.

## Concluding remarks

Cellular DNA-repair mechanisms are not mutually exclusive.And, just as repair enzymes can participate in different repair pathways, proteins with other functions in the cell can bind to DNA lesions and potentially influence repair. RPA, RNAPs and ATR contribute constructively towards repair through their recognition of damage, and the FA proteins seem to participate in a novel repair pathway.
